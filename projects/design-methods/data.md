# Design Methods

## Content Review Manuscript

> **Purpose of this document**
>
> This document contains pedagogical content for the Design Methods reference. It is intentionally independent of the application's data structure and programming logic.
>
> Each method is described through five components:
>
> **Description** — What the method is and what it helps a designer do.  
> **When to use** — Situations in which the method may be useful.  
> **Process** — A practical sequence for applying the method.  
> **Look out for** — Limitations, common mistakes and considerations.  
> **References** — Sources that inform the description and provide opportunities for further reading.
>
> The processes presented here are intended as starting points rather than fixed recipes. Design methods are adapted according to the project, participants, context and questions being investigated.

# A/B Testing or Split Testing

## Description

A/B testing compares two versions of a design to investigate whether a specific change affects user behaviour or another predefined measure. Participants encounter one of the alternatives and their responses are compared across the two conditions. The method is particularly useful when a design question can be expressed as a clear hypothesis and evaluated using observable measures.

A/B testing can help designers choose between alternatives, but it explains **which version performed differently**, not necessarily **why**. Qualitative research may therefore be useful alongside an A/B test.

## When to use

Use A/B testing when you have two clearly defined alternatives and enough participants or interactions to make a meaningful comparison. It is particularly useful for evaluating focused changes to an existing interface, service or communication rather than exploring an undefined design problem.

## Process

1. Identify the design decision you want to investigate.
2. Form a hypothesis describing the expected effect of the change.
3. Define a measurable outcome before running the test.
4. Create two versions that differ primarily in the feature being investigated.
5. Assign comparable participants or users to each version.
6. Collect the same measures for both versions.
7. Compare the results and consider whether the observed difference is meaningful.
8. Use the findings to inform the next design iteration or further investigation.

## Look out for

Avoid changing many things simultaneously, as this makes it difficult to determine what caused a difference.

Do not interpret a small numerical difference as evidence without considering sample size, variability and uncertainty.

Optimising a measurable behaviour does not necessarily mean that the design has become better overall. Consider accessibility, user experience, ethics and longer-term consequences alongside the selected metric.

## References

- Kohavi, R., Tang, D. & Xu, Y. (2020). _Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: A Practical Guide to A/B Testing_. Cambridge University Press.
- Nielsen Norman Group. _A/B Testing, Usability Testing, and Quantitative Studies_.

# Affinity Diagrams

## Description

Affinity diagramming is a collaborative synthesis method for organising a large collection of observations, ideas or pieces of research data into meaningful groups. Individual items are arranged according to relationships that emerge from the material rather than being forced immediately into predetermined categories.

As clusters develop, designers examine what connects the items and give each group a descriptive label. The resulting structure can help transform fragmented observations into themes, questions and areas for further investigation.

## When to use

Use affinity diagramming when a project has accumulated more observations or ideas than can easily be considered individually. It is particularly useful after interviews, observations, workshops or brainstorming sessions when a team needs to identify patterns across many pieces of information.

## Process

1. Break the material into individual observations, statements or ideas.
2. Place each item on a separate note or card.
3. Review the items without deciding categories in advance.
4. Move related items together based on similarities or relationships.
5. Discuss ambiguous items and revise clusters as patterns emerge.
6. Give each cluster a label that describes the underlying relationship rather than merely its topic.
7. Examine relationships between clusters and identify broader themes.
8. Record the resulting structure and the evidence contributing to each theme.

## Look out for

Affinity diagrams involve interpretation. A cluster is not automatically a research finding simply because several notes have been placed together.

Avoid vague headings such as "technology" or "problems" when a more analytical statement can explain what connects the observations.

Keep connections to the original research material so that themes can be checked against evidence.

## References

- Beyer, H. & Holtzblatt, K. (1998). _Contextual Design: Defining Customer-Centered Systems_. Morgan Kaufmann.
- Martin, B. & Hanington, B. (2012). _Universal Methods of Design_. Rockport.

# Autobiography / Diary

## Description

Autobiographical and diary approaches investigate experiences as they unfold over time. In diary studies, participants document selected activities, experiences, decisions or feelings over a period rather than attempting to reconstruct them later in an interview. In autobiographical design research, designers may systematically examine their own experiences as material for reflection and inquiry.

These approaches can reveal routines, changes and contextual details that are difficult to observe directly, particularly when experiences are private, intermittent or distributed across long periods.

## When to use

Use diary approaches when the phenomenon you are studying occurs over days or weeks, cannot easily be observed by a researcher, or is strongly influenced by context.

Autobiographical approaches can be useful for exploratory and reflective design work, particularly when the designer has direct experience of the situation being investigated.

## Process

1. Define what experiences or behaviours you want to understand.
2. Decide an appropriate period for documentation.
3. Create prompts that encourage useful observations without making participation unnecessarily burdensome.
4. Ask participants to record experiences close to when they occur.
5. Allow appropriate forms of documentation, such as writing, photographs, sketches, audio or screenshots.
6. Review entries during or after the study.
7. Conduct follow-up conversations where clarification is useful.
8. Analyse patterns, changes, exceptions and contextual influences across the material.

## Look out for

Diary studies require sustained participant effort and incomplete entries are common.

Frequent prompts can influence the behaviour being studied.

Personal records may contain sensitive or unexpected information, so consent, privacy and data handling require particular attention.

Autobiographical experience can produce valuable situated knowledge but should not automatically be treated as representative of other people's experiences.

## References

- Bolger, N., Davis, A. & Rafaeli, E. (2003). “Diary Methods: Capturing Life as It Is Lived.” _Annual Review of Psychology_, 54, 579–616.
- Neustaedter, C. & Sengers, P. (2012). “Autobiographical Design in HCI Research.” _Proceedings of DIS 2012_.

# Backcasting

## Description

Backcasting begins with a desirable or challenging future and works backwards to investigate how that future might be reached. Rather than extending current trends forward, participants first describe a future condition and then identify the changes, decisions, capabilities and intermediate states that would need to occur between that future and the present.

The method is useful for shifting attention from predicting what is likely to happen toward considering what would have to change for a preferred future to become possible.

## When to use

Use backcasting when a project involves long-term transformation, sustainability, policy, strategy or systemic change and incremental improvement of the present may be insufficient.

It works particularly well when a team has already developed a future scenario or vision but needs to connect that proposition to actions in the present.

## Process

1. Establish the issue and the time horizon being considered.
2. Describe a specific future condition or desired outcome.
3. Identify the important characteristics that distinguish this future from the present.
4. Work backwards to identify conditions that would need to exist immediately before that future.
5. Continue backwards through intermediate milestones until reaching the present.
6. Identify decisions, interventions and dependencies connecting the milestones.
7. Examine barriers, uncertainties and actors who could enable or prevent change.
8. Translate near-term findings into possible actions or experiments.

## Look out for

A desirable future for one group may be undesirable for another. Make assumptions and values explicit.

Backcasting does not demonstrate that a future will occur. It explores pathways under particular assumptions.

Avoid constructing a single inevitable pathway. Alternative routes and unexpected events should remain possible.

## References

- Robinson, J. B. (1990). “Futures Under Glass: A Recipe for People Who Hate to Predict.” _Futures_, 22(8), 820–842.
- Dreborg, K. H. (1996). “Essence of Backcasting.” _Futures_, 28(9), 813–828.

# Body Mapping

## Description

Body mapping uses an outline or representation of the body as a surface for recording physical sensations, emotions, experiences or relationships to an activity. Participants annotate, draw or otherwise mark locations on the body map in response to prompts.

The method can make embodied aspects of experience easier to discuss, particularly when those experiences are difficult to communicate through conventional verbal descriptions alone.

## When to use

Use body mapping when bodily sensation, movement, emotion, comfort, discomfort or physical interaction is important to the design question.

It can support research into experiences involving products, environments, healthcare, wearables, movement and embodied interaction.

## Process

1. Establish the aspect of experience you want participants to explore.
2. Provide an appropriate body outline or invite participants to create one.
3. Explain what different forms of annotation might represent without over-prescribing responses.
4. Ask participants to mark sensations, experiences, interactions or emotions on the map.
5. Invite participants to add words, symbols, colours or connections where useful.
6. Ask participants to explain their annotations in their own terms.
7. Document the completed maps and accompanying explanations.
8. Analyse both individual experiences and recurring patterns without losing their context.

## Look out for

Bodies and bodily experiences can be sensitive subjects. Participation should not require disclosure that makes participants uncomfortable.

Standardised body outlines may exclude or misrepresent different bodies and abilities.

Do not interpret colours, symbols or locations without asking participants what those markings mean to them.

## References

- Gastaldo, D., Magalhães, L., Carrasco, C. & Davy, C. (2012). _Body-Map Storytelling as Research_. University of Toronto.
- Martin, B. & Hanington, B. (2012). _Universal Methods of Design_. Rockport.

# Bodystorming

## Description

Bodystorming explores design ideas by physically acting within a situation rather than discussing it only in the abstract. Participants enact activities, interactions or scenarios using their bodies, spaces and simple props. Through performance, designers can notice spatial relationships, movement, timing, coordination and social interactions that may be difficult to anticipate through sketches alone.

The aim is not polished acting. It is to think through making and doing.

## When to use

Use bodystorming when physical movement, spatial arrangement, interpersonal interaction or sequences of activity are important to a design.

It is useful during early concept development because situations can be enacted and changed rapidly without producing detailed prototypes.

## Process

1. Select a situation, interaction or design question to explore.
2. Establish a simple physical setting using available spaces and props.
3. Assign roles to participants where necessary.
4. Act through the situation rather than describing what would happen.
5. Observe moments of confusion, effort, opportunity or unexpected behaviour.
6. Change one aspect of the interaction and enact it again.
7. Repeat several variations.
8. Record observations and translate useful discoveries into design ideas or questions.

## Look out for

Participants may perform what they think _should_ happen rather than what would occur in real use.

Bodystorming is an exploratory design activity rather than a substitute for research with relevant participants.

Ensure that physical participation is optional and provide alternative ways of contributing for people who cannot or do not wish to perform particular actions.

## References

- Oulasvirta, A., Kurvinen, E. & Kankainen, T. (2003). “Understanding Contexts by Being There: Case Studies in Bodystorming.” _Personal and Ubiquitous Computing_, 7, 125–134.
- Schleicher, D., Jones, P. & Kachur, O. (2010). “Bodystorming as Embodied Designing.” _Interactions_, 17(6), 47–51.

# Brainstorming

## Description

Brainstorming is a structured approach to generating many possible responses to a design question before evaluating them. Participants temporarily defer judgement, build on existing suggestions and deliberately pursue variety rather than immediately selecting the most practical idea.

The value of brainstorming lies in supporting divergent exploration. It should usually be followed by a separate process for organising, developing and evaluating ideas.

## When to use

Use brainstorming when a design problem has been sufficiently understood to support ideation but the team wants to expand the range of possible responses.

It can be useful at the beginning of concept development or whenever a project has converged prematurely around a small number of familiar solutions.

## Process

1. Frame a focused question or opportunity.
2. Establish a short period dedicated to idea generation.
3. Encourage participants to produce ideas without evaluating them immediately.
4. Record every contribution visibly.
5. Encourage participants to combine, extend and challenge existing ideas.
6. Introduce prompts or constraints if the group becomes repetitive.
7. Review the resulting ideas after the divergent phase has finished.
8. Cluster, develop or evaluate promising directions using appropriate criteria.

## Look out for

Speaking in a group does not give everyone an equal opportunity to contribute. Dominant voices, status and confidence can shape which ideas receive attention.

Individual idea generation before group discussion can reduce some of these effects.

Brainstorming cannot compensate for weak understanding of the problem. Generating more ideas from unsupported assumptions may simply generate more unsupported solutions.

## References

- Osborn, A. F. (1953). _Applied Imagination_. Charles Scribner's Sons.
- Paulus, P. B. & Nijstad, B. A. (eds.) (2003). _Group Creativity: Innovation Through Collaboration_. Oxford University Press.

# Business Model Canvas

## Description

The Business Model Canvas is a visual framework for describing how an organisation proposes, creates and captures value. It brings nine connected areas—including customers, value propositions, activities, resources, relationships, channels, partners, costs and revenue—onto a shared canvas.

For designers, it can help expose the organisational assumptions surrounding a proposed product or service and reveal dependencies that may not be visible when concentrating only on the user-facing experience.

## When to use

Use the Business Model Canvas when developing or examining a product, service or venture whose organisational model is still uncertain.

It is particularly useful for comparing alternative propositions and identifying assumptions that need further research or testing.

## Process

1. Define the product, service or organisation being examined.
2. Identify the people or organisations expected to receive value.
3. Describe the proposed value for those groups.
4. Map channels and relationships connecting the organisation with them.
5. Identify the activities, resources and partners required to deliver the proposition.
6. Describe important costs and possible sources of revenue or other forms of value.
7. Examine relationships and dependencies across the canvas.
8. Mark uncertain assumptions and identify how they could be investigated.

## Look out for

Completing every box does not validate a business model. Much of the canvas may initially consist of assumptions.

The framework can privilege commercial interpretations of value. Public, social and community projects may require broader definitions of value and exchange.

Treat the canvas as a model to interrogate rather than a form to complete.

## References

- Osterwalder, A. & Pigneur, Y. (2010). _Business Model Generation_. Wiley.

# Card Sorting

## Description

Card sorting investigates how people understand and group information. Participants organise cards representing topics, functions or content into categories that make sense to them. In an open card sort they create their own categories; in a closed card sort they place items into categories supplied by the researcher.

Patterns across participants can help designers understand mental models and inform information architecture, navigation and terminology.

## When to use

Use card sorting when you need to understand how people expect information or functions to be organised.

It is particularly useful when developing or revising the structure of websites, applications, services, documentation or other information-rich systems.

## Process

1. Define the information-organisation question.
2. Select representative items and write each on a separate card.
3. Decide whether an open, closed or hybrid card sort best fits the question.
4. Ask participants to group the cards according to what makes sense to them.
5. Ask participants to name groups when using an open sort.
6. Invite participants to explain difficult or ambiguous decisions.
7. Compare patterns across participants.
8. Use the findings alongside other research to develop or evaluate an information structure.

## Look out for

Card sorting investigates perceived relationships between labels; it does not reproduce the full experience of navigating an interface.

Results can be strongly affected by ambiguous card wording.

Do not assume that the most common grouping is automatically the best architecture. Important differences between participant groups may be hidden by aggregation.

## References

- Spencer, D. (2009). _Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories_. Rosenfeld Media.
- Rosenfeld, L., Morville, P. & Arango, J. (2015). _Information Architecture for the Web and Beyond_. O'Reilly.

# Cartographic Mapping

## Description

Cartographic mapping uses the conventions of maps to investigate relationships between people, activities, resources and places. Rather than treating location as background context, the method makes spatial relationships part of the analysis.

Maps may document existing conditions or become interpretive artefacts that reveal movement, access, boundaries, concentrations, absences and contested understandings of place.

## When to use

Use cartographic mapping when geography or spatial relationships materially influence the experience being designed.

It can support investigations of neighbourhoods, campuses, transport, public services, mobility, community resources and place-based experiences.

## Process

1. Define the geographic area and question being investigated.
2. Establish an appropriate base map or mapping surface.
3. Identify what information participants or researchers will record.
4. Map relevant locations, routes, events, resources or experiences.
5. Add annotations that explain why particular places or relationships matter.
6. Compare maps or layers where multiple perspectives are involved.
7. Look for patterns, gaps, boundaries and contradictions.
8. Use these observations to generate further questions or design opportunities.

## Look out for

Maps are representations rather than neutral descriptions of reality. Decisions about boundaries, scale and what is included shape what becomes visible.

Precise geographic data can create privacy risks.

Participatory mapping should allow people to represent places according to their own experiences rather than forcing every perspective into an official map.

## References

- Wood, D. (2010). _Rethinking the Power of Maps_. Guilford Press.
- Sanders, E. B.-N. & Stappers, P. J. (2012). _Convivial Toolbox_. BIS Publishers.

# Co-design Workshop

## Description

Co-design involves people affected by a design challenge participating directly in exploring and developing possible responses. A co-design workshop creates structured activities through which participants can share experiences, generate ideas, make representations and negotiate priorities.

The purpose is not simply to ask people whether they like a designer's proposal. Participants contribute knowledge and ideas to the design process itself.

## When to use

Use co-design when people with lived, professional or community experience should contribute directly to understanding and shaping a design situation.

It can be particularly valuable when a project affects multiple stakeholder groups or when designers need perspectives that cannot be generated from within the design team.

## Process

1. Clarify what participants can genuinely influence.
2. Identify whose perspectives need to be represented.
3. Design accessible activities that allow different forms of contribution.
4. Establish expectations, consent and an appropriate working environment.
5. Begin with activities that surface participants' experiences and perspectives.
6. Move toward collaborative exploration or making.
7. Document ideas as well as disagreements and uncertainties.
8. Explain how workshop outcomes will influence subsequent decisions.
9. Return outcomes to participants where appropriate.

## Look out for

Participation is not automatically equitable. Differences in authority, confidence, language, expertise and accessibility affect who can contribute.

Do not describe a workshop as co-design if important decisions have already been made and participants have little capacity to influence them.

Participation creates responsibilities around recognition, compensation, ownership and communicating what happens next.

## References

- Sanders, E. B.-N. & Stappers, P. J. (2008). “Co-creation and the New Landscapes of Design.” _CoDesign_, 4(1), 5–18.
- Sanders, E. B.-N. & Stappers, P. J. (2012). _Convivial Toolbox_. BIS Publishers.

# Competitor Analysis

## Description

Competitor analysis systematically compares existing products, services or organisations operating in a related area. Designers examine how alternatives address similar needs, communicate value, structure interactions or differentiate themselves.

The method can reveal established conventions, gaps and opportunities, but its purpose is not simply to imitate successful competitors.

## When to use

Use competitor analysis when entering an established product or service space, reviewing an existing offering or investigating how similar problems are currently addressed.

It can also be used more broadly to examine analogous experiences outside the immediate market.

## Process

1. Define what you want to learn from the comparison.
2. Select direct competitors and, where useful, analogous examples.
3. Establish consistent dimensions for comparison.
4. Gather evidence by experiencing or examining each example.
5. Record similarities, differences, strengths and limitations.
6. Identify recurring conventions and unusual approaches.
7. Consider what needs appear underserved.
8. Translate observations into questions or opportunities rather than copied features.

## Look out for

Competitors' design decisions are not evidence of user needs.

Focusing only on current competitors can encourage incremental rather than original thinking.

Publicly visible features reveal little about the organisational constraints, research or failures behind a product.

## References

- Porter, M. E. (1980). _Competitive Strategy_. Free Press.
- Martin, B. & Hanington, B. (2012). _Universal Methods of Design_. Rockport.

# Contextual Inquiry

## Description

Contextual inquiry combines observation and interviewing while a participant carries out an activity in its normal context. Instead of asking someone to recall a process away from the situation, the researcher observes the activity and asks questions as relevant events occur.

This can reveal tacit knowledge, workarounds, environmental constraints and relationships between people, tools and information that may be difficult to articulate in a conventional interview.

## When to use

Use contextual inquiry when understanding **how an activity is actually performed** is important to the design problem.

It is particularly useful for complex work practices, services, professional environments and activities involving multiple tools or interruptions.

## Process

1. Define the activities and contexts you need to understand.
2. Recruit participants with relevant experience.
3. Explain the researcher's observational role and establish consent.
4. Ask the participant to conduct the activity as naturally as practical.
5. Observe actions, tools, interruptions and environmental conditions.
6. Ask brief questions when clarification is needed.
7. Invite participants to explain decisions or unexpected actions.
8. Document observations and interpretations separately.
9. Synthesise patterns across sessions.

## Look out for

The presence of a researcher can alter behaviour.

Avoid interrupting so frequently that the activity becomes an interview rather than contextual observation.

Workplaces and homes may expose information belonging to people who have not consented to participate.

## References

- Beyer, H. & Holtzblatt, K. (1998). _Contextual Design: Defining Customer-Centered Systems_. Morgan Kaufmann.
- Holtzblatt, K., Wendell, J. B. & Wood, S. (2004). _Rapid Contextual Design_. Morgan Kaufmann.

# Contextual Observation

## Description

Contextual observation involves watching people, activities and environments in the setting where an experience naturally occurs. The researcher pays attention not only to what people say but also to actions, routines, artefacts, interruptions, spatial conditions and interactions with others.

Observation can expose differences between reported behaviour and situated practice and can reveal aspects of an experience that participants may consider too ordinary to mention.

## When to use

Use contextual observation when behaviour, environment or interactions are central to understanding a design situation.

It is particularly useful early in research when the team needs to understand an unfamiliar context before deciding which questions are important.

## Process

1. Define the setting and broad research focus.
2. Establish access, consent and appropriate boundaries.
3. Observe activities without attempting to document everything.
4. Record actions, sequences, environmental conditions and relevant interactions.
5. Separate direct observations from your interpretations.
6. Note unusual events as well as recurring patterns.
7. Ask clarifying questions where appropriate.
8. Review observations across sessions and identify questions requiring further investigation.

## Look out for

Observation shows behaviour but does not automatically explain people's motivations.

Researchers inevitably select what they notice and record.

Be particularly careful about privacy and incidental observation of people who are not research participants.

## References

- Blomberg, J., Burrell, M. & Guest, G. (2003). “An Ethnographic Approach to Design.” In Jacko, J. & Sears, A. (eds.), _The Human-Computer Interaction Handbook_. Lawrence Erlbaum.
- Martin, B. & Hanington, B. (2012). _Universal Methods of Design_. Rockport.

# Crazy 8s

## Description

Crazy 8s is a rapid sketching exercise in which participants generate multiple responses to the same design problem within a short period. A page is commonly divided into eight spaces and participants produce one idea in each.

The time and quantity constraints encourage designers to move beyond their first solution and explore variations without investing heavily in any single concept.

## When to use

Use Crazy 8s when a team understands the design problem but has converged too quickly on one solution or needs to generate alternative forms for a concept.

It works well as a short individual activity before group discussion.

## Process

1. Frame a focused design question.
2. Give each participant a page divided into eight spaces.
3. Set a short time limit.
4. Ask participants to sketch a different response in each space.
5. Encourage rough representations rather than polished drawings.
6. Review all eight ideas after the sketching period.
7. Ask participants to identify promising elements rather than simply selecting a winner.
8. Combine or develop those elements through subsequent sketches or prototypes.

## Look out for

Speed should encourage exploration, not anxiety. Adjust timing where necessary.

Drawing confidence should not determine whose ideas receive attention.

Eight superficial variations of the same idea do not necessarily represent meaningful divergence; encourage changes in underlying approach as well as appearance.

## References

- Google Ventures. _Design Sprint: Crazy 8s_.
- Knapp, J., Zeratsky, J. & Kowitz, B. (2016). _Sprint_. Simon & Schuster.

# Critical Speculation

## Description

Critical speculation uses designed propositions to question assumptions about technologies, systems, values and possible futures. Rather than proposing an immediately implementable solution, designers construct scenarios, artefacts or experiences that make an issue available for examination and debate.

The value of the work often lies in the questions and tensions it exposes rather than in whether the proposition should literally be produced.

## When to use

Use critical speculation when a project needs to examine assumptions, values or consequences that conventional solution-focused methods leave unchallenged.

It is particularly useful for emerging technologies, social change, contested futures and situations where asking "should this exist?" is as important as asking "how should we design it?"

## Process

1. Identify the issue, assumption or emerging condition to investigate.
2. Research the social and technological context surrounding it.
3. Identify a tension, contradiction or value that could be amplified.
4. Construct a plausible speculative premise.
5. Develop an artefact, scenario or experience that embodies that premise.
6. Consider how audiences will encounter and interpret the proposition.
7. Use the work to prompt discussion, reflection or research.
8. Analyse the responses and questions that emerge rather than treating the artefact itself as the final answer.

## Look out for

Provocation without a clear critical question can become novelty.

Speculative work often reflects the designer's own cultural assumptions about what counts as desirable, disturbing or plausible.

Distinguish clearly between speculation, prediction and recommendation.

## References

- Dunne, A. & Raby, F. (2013). _Speculative Everything: Design, Fiction, and Social Dreaming_. MIT Press.
- Auger, J. (2013). “Speculative Design: Crafting the Speculation.” _Digital Creativity_, 24(1), 11–35.

# Cultural Probes

## Description

Cultural probes are collections of open-ended activities given to participants to document aspects of their lives, experiences and environments. A probe might include prompts, postcards, maps, cameras, diaries or playful tasks.

Originally developed as an intentionally evocative design-research approach, probes are intended to generate rich and sometimes surprising material that can inspire design thinking rather than function as a conventional instrument for collecting standardised data.

## When to use

Use cultural probes when designers want exploratory access to experiences that are personal, dispersed over time or difficult to observe directly.

They are useful when ambiguity and participant interpretation are productive rather than when standardised answers are required.

## Process

1. Identify the broad area of experience you want to explore.
2. Develop a small set of engaging, open-ended activities.
3. Make the purpose and participation requirements understandable.
4. Invite participants to complete the activities in their own contexts.
5. Allow space for unexpected or idiosyncratic responses.
6. Collect the returned materials.
7. Review them individually and collectively.
8. Use the material to inspire questions, interpretations and design directions.
9. Follow up with participants where their interpretation is important.

## Look out for

Cultural probes were not originally intended as straightforward data-collection instruments. Avoid treating evocative responses as objective measurements.

Probe activities can demand considerable participant time.

Personal artefacts may reveal sensitive information, requiring careful consent and handling.

## References

- Gaver, B., Dunne, T. & Pacenti, E. (1999). “Design: Cultural Probes.” _Interactions_, 6(1), 21–29.
- Boehner, K., Vertesi, J., Sengers, P. & Dourish, P. (2007). “How HCI Interprets the Probes.” _CHI 2007_.

# Design Critique

## Description

Design critique is a structured conversation in which a design is examined against its intentions, context and relevant criteria. Participants describe what they observe, ask questions and offer reasoned feedback intended to help the work develop.

A critique differs from an informal expression of preference. Useful critique connects observations to design goals and explains the reasoning behind suggestions.

## When to use

Use critique throughout development when work is sufficiently tangible for others to examine but still open to change.

It is especially useful for exposing assumptions, comparing interpretations and helping designers see consequences they may have overlooked.

## Process

1. Clarify what is being designed and what feedback would be useful.
2. Present enough context for reviewers to understand the design's intentions.
3. Give participants time to examine the work before responding.
4. Begin with observations and questions.
5. Relate feedback to goals, evidence or design principles.
6. Distinguish problems from possible solutions.
7. Allow the designer to clarify without needing to defend every decision.
8. Record significant questions and observations.
9. Decide afterwards which feedback should influence the next iteration.

## Look out for

Personal taste is not the same as design reasoning.

Power relationships can make critique difficult, particularly when senior participants present opinions as instructions.

Avoid feedback that simply redesigns the work according to the reviewer's preferences.

## References

- Connor, A. & Irizarry, A. (2015). _Discussing Design: Improving Communication and Collaboration through Critique_. O'Reilly.

# Design Fiction

## Description

Design fiction explores possible worlds through fictional but tangible representations of products, services, systems or media. These artefacts are often presented as though they belong to another present or a possible future.

By making an imagined condition concrete enough to encounter and discuss, design fiction can help people examine how technologies might intersect with everyday life, institutions, behaviours and values.

## When to use

Use design fiction when abstract discussion of future possibilities is insufficient and a project would benefit from making assumptions or consequences tangible.

It is useful for emerging technologies, strategic exploration, futures work and critical discussion.

## Process

1. Identify the change or question to explore.
2. Research relevant technological and social conditions.
3. Develop a coherent fictional world or premise.
4. Decide what everyday artefact could provide a glimpse into that world.
5. Create the artefact with enough detail to suggest its surrounding context.
6. Consider secondary consequences: who uses it, who provides it and what institutions support it?
7. Present the fiction to an audience.
8. Use reactions and discussion to examine assumptions, consequences and alternative possibilities.

## Look out for

A futuristic-looking object is not automatically design fiction; the surrounding world matters.

Avoid presenting speculative propositions as forecasts.

Consider whose future is represented and whose experience is absent.

## References

- Bleecker, J. (2009). _Design Fiction: A Short Essay on Design, Science, Fact and Fiction_.
- Sterling, B. (2009). “Design Fiction.” _Interactions_, 16(3), 20–24.
- Dunne, A. & Raby, F. (2013). _Speculative Everything_. MIT Press.

# Design Matrix

## Description

A design matrix compares multiple concepts against a consistent set of criteria. Concepts are placed on one axis and criteria on another so that alternatives can be examined systematically.

The matrix helps make decision-making assumptions visible and can prevent teams from selecting concepts solely on intuition or enthusiasm. Its purpose is to structure judgement rather than produce an automatically correct answer.

## When to use

Use a design matrix when several concepts have been generated and the team needs a transparent way to compare their relative strengths, limitations and trade-offs.

## Process

1. Identify the concepts to compare.
2. Define criteria relevant to the project's goals and constraints.
3. Clarify what each criterion means.
4. Decide whether criteria require weighting.
5. Examine each concept against each criterion.
6. Record scores or qualitative judgements together with supporting reasoning.
7. Compare patterns across the matrix.
8. Investigate surprising results and trade-offs.
9. Use the matrix to inform discussion rather than allowing totals alone to make the decision.

## Look out for

Numbers can give subjective judgements an appearance of objectivity.

Criteria and weighting can predetermine which concepts appear successful.

Important qualities may be difficult to reduce to scores. Record reasoning alongside numerical ratings.

## References

- Pugh, S. (1991). _Total Design: Integrated Methods for Successful Product Engineering_. Addison-Wesley.

# Design Metaphor

## Description

Design metaphors use the characteristics of one domain to help imagine or explain another. A designer might ask how a service could behave like a library, ecosystem, conversation or marketplace and use relationships from that source domain to generate new possibilities.

Metaphors can support ideation and communication by making unfamiliar concepts easier to think about through familiar structures.

## When to use

Use design metaphors when a team is struggling to move beyond existing conventions, when an abstract system needs to become easier to understand, or when alternative interaction models are being explored.

## Process

1. Define the design problem or experience.
2. Identify its important qualities or relationships.
3. Generate several unrelated metaphorical domains.
4. Select a metaphor and examine its components, behaviours and relationships.
5. Map potentially useful relationships onto the design problem.
6. Generate concepts from those mappings.
7. Repeat with contrasting metaphors.
8. Evaluate which resulting ideas are useful without forcing the final design to follow the metaphor literally.

## Look out for

Metaphors highlight some relationships while hiding others.

A familiar metaphor may carry assumptions that do not fit the design situation.

Do not let the metaphor become a constraint once it stops being productive.

## References

- Lakoff, G. & Johnson, M. (1980). _Metaphors We Live By_. University of Chicago Press.
- Erickson, T. D. (1990). “Working with Interface Metaphors.” In Laurel, B. (ed.), _The Art of Human-Computer Interface Design_. Addison-Wesley.

# Design Timescape

## Description

A design timescape is a visual futures-thinking method for examining relationships between design propositions and broader changes over time. It places developments, events, trends and possible interventions along a temporal landscape so designers can consider how a proposal might interact with changing social, technological, environmental or political conditions.

Rather than treating a design as an isolated future object, the method encourages designers to consider the changing context around it.

## When to use

Use a design timescape when investigating a design proposition whose relevance or consequences may change over a longer time horizon.

It is particularly useful in design-futures work and when introducing students to relationships between design decisions and societal change.

## Process

1. Define the issue and time horizon.
2. Establish the present conditions relevant to the issue.
3. Identify significant historical developments that shaped those conditions.
4. Research trends, signals and anticipated changes.
5. Place these developments across a shared timeline.
6. Add possible design interventions or propositions.
7. Examine relationships, tensions and dependencies across the timescape.
8. Use the resulting landscape to construct arguments about when, why or under what conditions particular design responses might matter.

## Look out for

A timescape should not imply that the future is a single predictable timeline.

Separate known events, current trends, assumptions and speculative possibilities.

Consider social and political change alongside technological developments.

## References

- Cooper, C. (2025). “Futuring Visual Communication Pedagogy: Two Visual Thinking Tools for Applied Design Futuring.” _Journal of Futures Studies_, 30(2).
- Tomitsch, M. et al. (2021). _Design. Think. Make. Break. Repeat: A Handbook of Methods_, 2nd ed. BIS Publishers.

# Ecology Mapping

## Description

Ecology mapping visualises the network of people, organisations, technologies, resources and relationships surrounding a design situation. Rather than examining a product or user in isolation, the map helps designers see the wider ecology in which experiences and outcomes are produced.

The emphasis is on relationships: who or what influences whom, what resources move through the system and where dependencies or tensions occur.

## When to use

Use ecology mapping when a design challenge involves many interconnected actors or when focusing on a single user-product relationship obscures important influences.

## Process

1. Define the situation or experience being mapped.
2. Identify relevant human and non-human actors.
3. Place the focal issue or experience on the mapping surface.
4. Add actors, organisations, technologies and resources around it.
5. Draw relationships between them.
6. Annotate important flows, dependencies, conflicts or exchanges.
7. Identify missing actors and indirect influences.
8. Examine the map for leverage points, tensions and questions requiring further research.

## Look out for

A visually complex map is not necessarily an insightful map.

Avoid treating every connection as equally important.

The map reflects the knowledge and perspective of whoever created it; validate important relationships through research.

- Young, I. (2008). _Mental Models_. Rosenfeld Media.

# Empathy Mapping

## Description

An empathy map is a synthesis tool used to organise what is known or hypothesised about a person's experience. Common versions consider what a person says, does, thinks and feels, although the exact structure varies.

Used carefully, the map can help a team bring scattered research observations together and notice contradictions or gaps in its understanding.

## When to use

Use empathy mapping after collecting research about people's experiences and when a team needs a shared representation to support discussion or identify unanswered questions.

## Process

1. Define the person, group or situation represented.
2. Gather relevant research evidence.
3. Add observations and quotations to appropriate areas of the map.
4. Distinguish directly observed evidence from interpretation.
5. Discuss contradictions between what people say, do and appear to experience.
6. Identify important needs, difficulties or goals supported by the evidence.
7. Mark areas where evidence is missing.
8. Use these gaps to inform further research.

## Look out for

Designers cannot directly know what another person thinks or feels.

Do not populate empathy maps primarily from imagination and then present them as user research.

Avoid stereotypes and unsupported psychological claims.

- Gray, D., Brown, S. & Macanufo, J. (2010). _Gamestorming_. O'Reilly.
- Nielsen Norman Group. _Empathy Mapping: The First Step in Design Thinking_.

# Five Whys

## Description

Five Whys is a questioning technique for moving beyond an immediately visible problem by repeatedly asking why a condition occurs. Each response becomes the basis for another question, helping participants trace possible contributing causes.

The number five is a prompt rather than a rule. The purpose is to investigate causal assumptions rather than stop at the first explanation.

## When to use

Use Five Whys when a team is reacting to a symptom and needs to investigate what conditions might be producing it.

It works best for relatively focused problems and as an entry point to deeper investigation.

## Process

1. State the observed problem as specifically as possible.
2. Ask why it occurs.
3. Record the proposed explanation.
4. Ask why that condition occurs.
5. Continue following the causal chain.
6. Check each explanation against available evidence.
7. Branch the analysis where several causes are plausible.
8. Identify causes that can be investigated or addressed.

## Look out for

Complex problems rarely have a single linear root cause.

Teams may produce explanations based on assumption rather than evidence.

The method can inadvertently focus blame on individuals when organisational or systemic conditions are more important.

## References

- Ohno, T. (1988). _Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production_. Productivity Press.

# Focus Groups

## Description

A focus group is a moderated group discussion used to explore participants' perceptions, experiences, language and responses to a topic. Interaction between participants is part of the method: people can agree, disagree, elaborate on one another's comments and expose different perspectives.

Focus groups are useful for understanding discussion around a topic, but they do not provide the same depth of individual experience as one-to-one interviews.

## When to use

Use focus groups when interaction between participants is valuable, when you want to explore a range of perspectives efficiently, or when social norms and shared understandings are relevant to the research question.

## Process

1. Define the research questions.
2. Recruit participants whose experiences are relevant.
3. Develop open-ended discussion prompts.
4. Establish expectations and confidentiality limitations.
5. Facilitate discussion without allowing one participant to dominate.
6. Probe differences and ask for examples.
7. Observe interaction as well as individual statements.
8. Record and analyse recurring themes, disagreements and minority perspectives.

## Look out for

Group pressure can suppress unpopular or sensitive opinions.

A confident participant can disproportionately shape discussion.

Focus groups are generally unsuitable for estimating how common an opinion is in a population.

Confidentiality cannot be guaranteed in the same way as an individual interview because other participants hear what is said.

## References

- Krueger, R. A. & Casey, M. A. (2015). _Focus Groups: A Practical Guide for Applied Research_, 5th ed. Sage.

# Futures Cone

## Description

The Futures Cone is a framework for distinguishing different kinds of future possibility. Futures are commonly considered in terms such as possible, plausible, probable and preferable, widening outward from the present.

The framework helps designers avoid treating a single imagined future as inevitable and encourages exploration of a broader space of alternatives.

## When to use

Use the Futures Cone when beginning futures-oriented design work, comparing scenarios or helping participants distinguish prediction from possibility and preference.

## Process

1. Define the issue and time horizon.
2. Identify assumptions about what is likely to continue.
3. Generate developments that appear probable.
4. Expand outward to plausible futures that could reasonably occur.
5. Explore possible futures requiring more substantial change.
6. Discuss preferable futures and ask preferable for whom.
7. Develop contrasting scenarios across the cone.
8. Examine implications for decisions in the present.

## Look out for

The boundaries between probable, plausible and possible are judgements rather than objective facts.

A preferable future is always value-laden.

Do not use the cone to imply increasing probability simply because a future is visually closer to the centre.

## References

- Voros, J. (2003). “A Generic Foresight Process Framework.” _Foresight_, 5(3), 10–21.
- Hancock, T. & Bezold, C. (1994). “Possible Futures, Preferable Futures.” _Healthcare Forum Journal_, 37(2), 23–29.

# Impact Ripple Canvas

## Description

The Impact Ripple Canvas helps designers explore consequences that may extend beyond the immediate intended effects of a design. Starting with a proposed intervention, participants consider how its effects might spread outward through people, organisations, communities, systems and time.

The method encourages consideration of indirect and unintended consequences rather than evaluating a proposal only according to its intended users and outcomes.

## When to use

Use the Impact Ripple Canvas when evaluating a concept, product, service or intervention that could have wider social, environmental or organisational effects.

It is particularly useful before implementation, when there is still an opportunity to redesign in response to potential consequences.

## Process

1. Place the proposed design or intervention at the centre.
2. Identify its intended immediate effects.
3. Identify people and systems directly affected.
4. Consider secondary effects produced by those changes.
5. Continue outward to more distant or longer-term consequences.
6. Include positive, negative and uncertain impacts.
7. Identify groups that may experience benefits and harms differently.
8. Prioritise significant consequences for further research or mitigation.
9. Revisit the design in response to the analysis.

## Look out for

The canvas identifies possibilities, not proven future impacts.

Teams tend to imagine consequences that fit their existing worldview, so include diverse perspectives.

Do not limit analysis to intended users; people who never interact directly with the design may still be affected.

## References

- Tomitsch, M. et al. (2021). _Design. Think. Make. Break. Repeat: A Handbook of Methods_, 2nd ed. BIS Publishers.

# Interaction Relabelling

## Description

Interaction relabelling is an ideation method in which the interactions afforded by an existing object are reinterpreted as interactions with a different product or system. Designers explore how actions such as turning, sliding, squeezing, balancing or unfolding might suggest alternative ways of interacting with the thing being designed.

The method deliberately shifts attention away from familiar interface conventions toward the expressive and physical qualities of interaction.

## When to use

Use interaction relabelling when developing interactive products and when existing conventions are constraining the range of ideas being considered.

It is particularly useful for exploring tangible and embodied interaction.

## Process

1. Select the product or interaction problem.
2. Choose an unrelated physical object with interesting interactions.
3. Explore how the object is held, moved and manipulated.
4. List the actions and feedback the object affords.
5. Temporarily reinterpret these actions as functions of the product being designed.
6. Generate interaction concepts from these mappings.
7. Repeat with contrasting objects.
8. Develop promising interactions beyond the original analogy.

## Look out for

The exercise is intentionally generative rather than evidence about what users need.

Avoid choosing only objects whose interactions already resemble the target product.

Novel interaction is not automatically better interaction; promising concepts still require evaluation for usability, accessibility and appropriateness.

## References

- Djajadiningrat, J. P., Gaver, W. W. & Frens, J. W. (2000). “Interaction Relabelling and Extreme Characters: Methods for Exploring Aesthetic Interactions.” _Proceedings of DIS 2000_, 66–71. DOI: 10.1145/347642.347664.
- Tomitsch, M. et al. (2021). _Design. Think. Make. Break. Repeat_. BIS Publishers.

# Interviews

## Description

Interviews are guided conversations used to investigate people's experiences, practices, perspectives and interpretations. In design research, semi-structured interviews are particularly common: the researcher prepares areas to explore while allowing participants' responses to shape the direction of the conversation.

Interviews are valuable for understanding how people make sense of their experiences, but what people report should not automatically be treated as a complete record of what they actually do.

## When to use

Use interviews when people's experiences, motivations, interpretations or decision-making are important to the design question.

They can be used early to explore an unfamiliar problem or later to investigate questions emerging from other research.

## Process

1. Define what you need to understand.
2. Recruit participants with relevant experience.
3. Develop a short guide of open-ended questions.
4. Establish informed consent.
5. Begin with accessible questions about concrete experiences.
6. Ask participants for examples rather than relying only on general opinions.
7. Probe interesting or contradictory responses.
8. Record or document the conversation appropriately.
9. Analyse interviews across participants for patterns and differences.

## Look out for

Leading questions can shape responses.

Participants may describe what they remember, believe or consider socially appropriate rather than exactly what occurred.

Avoid treating a small set of interviews as statistically representative of a population.

## References

- Kvale, S. & Brinkmann, S. (2009). _InterViews: Learning the Craft of Qualitative Research Interviewing_, 2nd ed. Sage.
- Rubin, H. J. & Rubin, I. S. (2012). _Qualitative Interviewing_, 3rd ed. Sage.

# Laddering

## Description

Laddering is an interviewing technique that moves between concrete attributes and deeper reasons, consequences or values by repeatedly asking participants why something matters. The researcher begins with a specific preference, feature or behaviour and progressively explores its significance.

The resulting chains can help designers understand connections between product characteristics, experiences and personal values.

## When to use

Use laddering when you need to understand why particular features, choices or experiences matter to people rather than simply knowing which option they prefer.

## Process

1. Begin with a concrete choice, attribute or experience.
2. Ask the participant why it is important.
3. Ask what consequence follows from that answer.
4. Continue asking why that consequence matters.
5. Record the chain from concrete attribute toward broader value.
6. Explore alternative branches where appropriate.
7. Repeat across several relevant attributes.
8. Compare patterns across participants.

## Look out for

Repeatedly asking "why?" can become uncomfortable or interrogative. Vary the phrasing and respect participants' boundaries.

Do not assume every preference has a single deep underlying value.

The interviewer's expectations can influence where a ladder appears to end.

## References

- Reynolds, T. J. & Gutman, J. (1988). “Laddering Theory, Method, Analysis, and Interpretation.” _Journal of Advertising Research_, 28(1), 11–31.

# Low-Fidelity Prototyping

## Description

Low-fidelity prototyping creates deliberately simple representations of a design so that ideas can be explored before substantial resources are committed to implementation. Prototypes may use paper, cardboard, rough digital screens, simple models or improvised materials.

Their value comes from making ideas tangible enough to examine, change and test while signalling that the design remains unfinished.

## When to use

Use low-fidelity prototypes early and repeatedly when the important questions concern structure, sequence, interaction or overall concept rather than visual polish or technical performance.

## Process

1. Identify the question the prototype needs to investigate.
2. Decide which aspects of the experience must be represented.
3. Select the simplest materials capable of representing those aspects.
4. Build only enough detail to support the investigation.
5. Walk through or test the prototype.
6. Observe where assumptions fail or new questions emerge.
7. Modify the prototype rapidly.
8. Repeat until the important uncertainties require a different level of fidelity.

## Look out for

Fidelity should match the question being investigated.

A rough prototype cannot reliably answer questions about qualities it does not represent, such as precise timing, visual appeal or physical durability.

Avoid spending time polishing a prototype whose purpose is rapid learning.

## References

- Houde, S. & Hill, C. (1997). “What Do Prototypes Prototype?” In Helander, M., Landauer, T. & Prabhu, P. (eds.), _Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction_. Elsevier.
- Buxton, B. (2007). _Sketching User Experiences_. Morgan Kaufmann.

# Material Exploration

## Description

Material exploration investigates design possibilities through direct engagement with materials and making processes. Designers manipulate, combine, test and transform materials to understand qualities that may not be apparent from specifications or images alone.

The process can generate knowledge about texture, flexibility, weight, resistance, sound, fabrication and sensory experience while simultaneously suggesting new design possibilities.

## When to use

Use material exploration when physical qualities materially affect the design or when making itself can contribute to discovering what the design could become.

## Process

1. Identify a broad material or experiential question.
2. Gather a diverse range of relevant materials.
3. Handle, manipulate and compare their properties.
4. Cut, join, deform or combine materials in different ways.
5. Produce small experimental samples rather than finished objects.
6. Document unexpected behaviours and sensory qualities.
7. Relate these discoveries to the design problem.
8. Develop promising experiments into more focused prototypes.

## Look out for

Material properties are affected by fabrication, scale and context.

Consider safety, sourcing, waste, repairability and environmental impact alongside aesthetic qualities.

Do not restrict exploration to materials already familiar to the design team.

## References

- Karana, E., Pedgley, O. & Rognoli, V. (eds.) (2014). _Materials Experience: Fundamentals of Materials and Design_. Butterworth-Heinemann.

# Mind Mapping

## Description

Mind mapping is a visual technique for expanding and organising ideas around a central topic. Related concepts branch outward from a central node, allowing associations, subtopics and relationships to become visible on a single surface.

It can support individual thinking or collaborative exploration and is particularly useful before the structure of a problem is fully understood.

## When to use

Use mind mapping when exploring the scope of a topic, organising early research questions or generating associations around a design challenge.

## Process

1. Write the central topic or question in the middle.
2. Add major related ideas as branches.
3. Extend each branch with more specific concepts.
4. Add connections between branches where relationships emerge.
5. Continue until significant areas and gaps become visible.
6. Review the map for clusters, contradictions and missing perspectives.
7. Reorganise it if a clearer structure emerges.
8. Use the map to identify research questions or areas for further development.

## Look out for

A mind map records associations, not necessarily evidence.

The structure can reflect the assumptions of the person who created it.

Avoid treating the first hierarchy that emerges as the definitive structure of the problem.

## References

- Buzan, T. & Buzan, B. (1993). _The Mind Map Book_. BBC Books.

# Mock-ups

## Description

A mock-up is a representation used to explore or communicate the appearance, arrangement or physical form of a proposed design. Mock-ups range from rough representations to highly realistic models, but unlike fully functional prototypes they often reproduce only selected aspects of the intended design.

They allow designers and stakeholders to encounter a proposal more concretely than they can through verbal description alone.

## When to use

Use mock-ups when questions concern visual hierarchy, scale, spatial arrangement, physical form or presentation and full functionality is unnecessary.

## Process

1. Identify what the mock-up needs to communicate or test.
2. Determine an appropriate scale and level of detail.
3. Select materials or software suited to those questions.
4. Construct the relevant visual or physical features.
5. Place the mock-up in an appropriate context where possible.
6. Review it from relevant viewpoints.
7. Gather feedback against the intended design goals.
8. Revise or increase fidelity only when required.

## Look out for

High visual fidelity can cause people to assume that underlying decisions are final.

A mock-up may look convincing while leaving important interaction or technical questions unresolved.

Make clear which aspects are represented accurately and which are placeholders.

- Hallgrimsson, B. (2012). _Prototyping and Modelmaking for Product Design_. Laurence King.

# Mood Boards

## Description

A mood board is a curated collection of images, colours, materials, typography, textures and other references assembled to explore the intended experiential or aesthetic direction of a design.

Mood boards can help teams discuss qualities that are difficult to communicate verbally and compare different interpretations before committing to a detailed visual language.

## When to use

Use mood boards when exploring visual, material or experiential direction and when collaborators need a shared vocabulary for qualities such as atmosphere, character or tone.

## Process

1. Define the experience or qualities being explored.
2. Gather a broad range of visual and material references.
3. Avoid limiting references to products identical to the one being designed.
4. Group references that express related qualities.
5. Curate the collection to create a coherent direction.
6. Annotate important qualities where necessary.
7. Compare alternative boards or directions.
8. Translate selected qualities into design decisions rather than copying individual references.

## Look out for

A mood board should communicate more than a fashionable aesthetic.

Avoid uncritical use of copyrighted imagery in published work.

Ensure that visual references do not introduce cultural stereotypes or associations that have not been considered.

# Morphological Chart

## Description

A morphological chart generates concepts by separating a design problem into important functions or dimensions and listing alternative ways of addressing each one. Designers then combine options from different rows to construct new concept configurations.

The method encourages systematic exploration of combinations that may not arise through free-form ideation.

## When to use

Use morphological charts when a design problem contains several relatively independent functions or characteristics and you want to explore combinations systematically.

## Process

1. Define the overall design problem.
2. Break it into important functions or dimensions.
3. Place each function on a separate row.
4. Generate multiple alternatives for each function.
5. Combine one option from each row to form a concept.
6. Explore unusual as well as obvious combinations.
7. Sketch or model promising configurations.
8. Evaluate developed concepts against project requirements.

## Look out for

The quality of the resulting concepts depends heavily on how the problem is decomposed.

Not every combination will be technically or conceptually coherent.

Avoid using the chart merely to generate a large number of combinations; develop those that reveal genuinely different approaches.

## References

- Zwicky, F. (1969). _Discovery, Invention, Research through the Morphological Approach_. Macmillan.

# Online Ethnography

## Description

Online ethnography adapts ethnographic approaches to digitally mediated communities, platforms and practices. Researchers examine interactions, norms, language, artefacts and behaviours within online contexts while considering how those spaces connect with participants' wider lives.

Depending on the research question, this may involve observation, participation, interviews or analysis of digital materials.

## When to use

Use online ethnography when the activity or community being investigated substantially exists through digital platforms or when online interactions are an important part of a broader social practice.

## Process

1. Define the community, practice or digital context.
2. Understand the norms and expectations of the space.
3. Determine appropriate ethical and consent procedures.
4. Observe interactions and recurring practices over time.
5. Record contextual information rather than collecting isolated posts.
6. Participate or interview where appropriate to the research design.
7. Examine relationships between platform features and social behaviour.
8. Analyse patterns while preserving relevant context.

## Look out for

Public accessibility does not automatically make online content ethically unrestricted research data.

Quoting searchable text can identify participants even after usernames are removed.

Platform populations and behaviours should not automatically be generalised to wider populations.

# Persona Walkthroughs

## Description

A persona walkthrough examines a proposed design by stepping through an activity from the perspective represented by a persona. At each stage, designers consider the persona's goals, circumstances, capabilities and likely questions.

The method can help teams expose mismatches between a concept and previously identified user needs, provided that the persona itself is grounded in credible research.

## When to use

Use persona walkthroughs when evaluating early flows, scenarios or concepts and when research-based personas already exist.

## Process

1. Select an appropriate research-based persona.
2. Define the task or scenario to examine.
3. Establish the persona's relevant goals and context.
4. Move through the design one step at a time.
5. At each point, ask what the persona knows, expects and needs.
6. Record barriers, uncertainties and unsupported assumptions.
7. Repeat with personas representing meaningfully different circumstances.
8. Use the findings to revise the design or identify questions for user research.

## Look out for

A walkthrough does not turn a fictional representation into an actual participant.

Weak or stereotypical personas will produce weak walkthroughs.

Do not use persona walkthroughs as a substitute for accessibility evaluation or research with relevant users.

## References

- Cooper, A. (1999). _The Inmates Are Running the Asylum_. Sams.

# Personas

## Description

Personas are research-informed representations of patterns found among people relevant to a design project. They typically bring together goals, behaviours, contexts and needs into a memorable character that teams can use when discussing design decisions.

A persona is a synthesis artefact rather than a research method in itself. Its usefulness depends on the quality of the evidence and analysis from which it was constructed.

## When to use

Use personas when substantial research has revealed meaningful patterns in goals or behaviours and a team needs a shared representation of those patterns during design.

## Process

1. Gather relevant user research.
2. Identify recurring behavioural and goal-related patterns.
3. Distinguish meaningful differences from superficial demographics.
4. Group related patterns into coherent behavioural archetypes.
5. Construct a concise persona for each important pattern.
6. Include evidence-relevant goals, behaviours and contexts.
7. Communicate where the persona came from and what it does not represent.
8. Revisit personas as new research becomes available.

## Look out for

Personas created from imagination can easily reproduce stereotypes.

Decorative details can make a persona memorable while adding little design value.

Do not imply that one persona represents every person within a demographic group.

# Pop-up Installation

## Description

A pop-up installation temporarily places a designed intervention in a real or realistic environment so that people can encounter, use or respond to it. Unlike a laboratory prototype, the intervention becomes part of the surrounding context for a limited period.

This can help designers investigate how a proposition interacts with place, existing activities and people who may not have been recruited specifically for a conventional study.

## When to use

Use a pop-up installation when spatial context, public interaction or situated experience is central to the concept and a temporary deployment is feasible.

2. Select an appropriate site.
3. Determine permissions, safety and accessibility requirements.
4. Create the minimum intervention needed to produce the intended encounter.
5. Install it for an appropriate period.
6. Observe how people encounter and interpret it.

8. Document unexpected uses and contextual effects.
9. Remove the installation and analyse the findings.

## Look out for

Public deployment introduces safety, consent, privacy and accessibility responsibilities.

People encountering the installation may not know they are part of a research activity.

Novelty effects can strongly influence behaviour during short deployments.

# Provocative Artifacts

## Description

Provocative artefacts are deliberately designed objects or representations intended to elicit reflection, discussion or reaction rather than simply demonstrate a solution. By embodying an unusual, uncomfortable or exaggerated proposition, the artefact can make assumptions and values easier to examine.

The responses it produces can become material for design research or critical inquiry.

## When to use

Use provocative artefacts when direct questioning is unlikely to expose assumptions or when a tangible proposition can help participants engage with abstract, contested or future-oriented issues.

## Process

1. Identify the assumption or issue to investigate.
2. Decide what kind of response would be useful to elicit.
3. Develop a proposition that makes the issue tangible.
4. Create an artefact with enough coherence to be interpreted.
5. Introduce it with an appropriate amount of context.
6. Observe initial reactions.
7. Ask participants to explain their interpretations and concerns.
8. Analyse the discussion rather than treating reactions as simple approval or rejection.

## Look out for

Provocation should serve an inquiry rather than exist only to shock.

Consider emotional consequences for participants before introducing disturbing material.

Participants may interpret the artefact very differently from the designer's intention; those differences are often important rather than errors to correct.

# Questionnaires

## Description

Questionnaires collect structured responses from participants through a consistent set of questions. They can gather quantitative measures, categorical responses and shorter qualitative comments from larger numbers of people than many interview-based approaches.

Their strength is consistency and reach rather than depth. Good questionnaire design depends on clearly defined research questions, careful sampling and questions that participants can interpret reliably.

## When to use

Use questionnaires when you need comparable responses from many participants, want to measure defined attitudes or behaviours, or need to investigate the prevalence of patterns identified through earlier qualitative research.

## Process

1. Define the research questions and target population.
2. Decide how participants will be sampled.
3. Write questions that each address a clear purpose.
4. Select response formats appropriate to the information required.
5. Avoid leading, double-barrelled and ambiguous questions.
6. Pilot the questionnaire with people similar to the intended participants.
7. Revise unclear questions.
8. Distribute the questionnaire consistently.
9. Analyse results in relation to the sampling method and limitations.

## Look out for

A large number of responses does not compensate for a biased sample.

Question wording and response scales can substantially influence answers.

Avoid asking participants to provide precision they cannot reasonably know or remember.

## References

- Dillman, D. A., Smyth, J. D. & Christian, L. M. (2014). _Internet, Phone, Mail, and Mixed-Mode Surveys_, 4th ed. Wiley.
- Fowler, F. J. (2014). _Survey Research Methods_, 5th ed. Sage.

# Reframing

## Description

Reframing deliberately changes how a design problem is understood. Instead of accepting the initial problem statement as fixed, designers examine assumptions, boundaries and perspectives and construct alternative ways of describing the situation.

A productive reframe can reveal different questions and therefore different spaces for design intervention.

## When to use

Use reframing when a project appears stuck, when solutions repeatedly address symptoms rather than underlying issues, or when the initial brief contains assumptions that have not been examined.

## Process

1. Write the current problem framing explicitly.
2. Identify assumptions embedded in its language.
3. Ask who defines the problem and who experiences it differently.
4. Examine what has been included and excluded.
5. Rewrite the problem from alternative perspectives.
6. Change scale, timescale or unit of analysis.
7. Generate design questions from several frames.
8. Compare what each frame makes visible and invisible.
9. Select or combine frames that open productive avenues for investigation.

## Look out for

Reframing should not be used to redefine away inconvenient evidence or stakeholder concerns.

Different frames carry different values and consequences.

A compelling reframe still requires investigation rather than becoming a new unquestioned assumption.

## References

- Schön, D. A. (1983). _The Reflective Practitioner_. Basic Books.
- Dorst, K. (2015). _Frame Innovation_. MIT Press.

# Roleplay

## Description

Roleplay explores a situation by having participants enact the roles, actions and interactions involved. It can make social dynamics, sequences and service encounters tangible before they are fully implemented.

By performing an interaction rather than only describing it, designers can discover practical and interpersonal issues that are difficult to anticipate in diagrams.

## When to use

Use roleplay when interpersonal interaction, service delivery, communication or coordination between actors is important to a design.

## Process

1. Define the scenario to explore.
2. Identify the relevant roles.
3. Provide participants with enough context to begin.
4. Establish simple props or environmental cues if needed.
5. Act through the scenario.
6. Pause or repeat when significant issues emerge.
7. Exchange roles or alter conditions to explore alternatives.
8. Debrief participants about what they noticed.
9. Translate observations into design questions or changes.

## Look out for

Roleplay reflects participants' assumptions about other people and should not be treated as evidence of how those people actually behave.

Some participants may be uncomfortable performing.

Provide alternative roles such as observer, documenter or facilitator where appropriate.

## References

- Simsarian, K. T. (2003). “Take It to the Next Stage: The Roles of Role Playing in the Design Process.” _CHI 2003 Extended Abstracts_.

# Scenarios

## Description

Scenarios are concise narratives describing how a person or group might encounter and use a design within a particular situation. They connect users, goals, context and sequences of activity, allowing designers to consider an experience over time rather than as isolated interface elements.

Scenarios may describe existing situations or explore proposed futures.

## When to use

Use scenarios when developing or evaluating how a concept fits into people's activities and when a sequence needs to be understood before detailed interaction design.

## Process

1. Define the person or actor involved.
2. Establish a specific context and goal.
3. Describe the situation that triggers the interaction.
4. Narrate the significant actions and responses.
5. Include contextual constraints and interruptions where relevant.
6. Identify points where the design supports or obstructs the goal.
7. Develop alternative scenarios representing different conditions.
8. Use the narratives to inform requirements, flows or prototypes.

## Look out for

Scenarios can easily become idealised stories in which everything works as the designer hopes.

Ground important scenarios in research.

Include edge conditions and interruptions rather than modelling only the happy path.

## References

- Carroll, J. M. (2000). _Making Use: Scenario-Based Design of Human-Computer Interactions_. MIT Press.

# Science Fiction Prototyping

## Description

Science fiction prototyping uses fictional narratives to explore how a technology or design proposition might operate within a future social context. Rather than describing a technology in isolation, the story considers people using, adapting to or resisting it.

The narrative becomes a prototype of relationships and consequences rather than a prediction of what will happen.

## When to use

Use science fiction prototyping when emerging technologies need to be considered beyond technical capability and when social adoption, everyday use or longer-term consequences are important.

## Process

1. Identify the technology or change to investigate.
2. Establish a plausible future context.
3. Introduce a character or community affected by the development.
4. Describe an everyday situation in which the technology matters.
5. Introduce a conflict, limitation or unexpected consequence.
6. Explore how people and institutions respond.
7. Reflect on what the story reveals about the original proposition.
8. Use those insights to revise assumptions or generate alternative futures.

## Look out for

Avoid technological determinism: technologies do not independently produce social outcomes.

A compelling story is not evidence that a future is probable.

Consider perspectives beyond the intended adopter or heroic innovator.

## References

- Johnson, B. D. (2011). _Science Fiction Prototyping: Designing the Future with Science Fiction_. Morgan & Claypool.

# Service Blueprint

## Description

A service blueprint visualises how a service is produced across customer-facing interactions and the activities occurring behind them. It commonly aligns user actions, touchpoints, frontstage staff activity, backstage processes and supporting systems along a timeline.

The blueprint helps teams see dependencies between the experience people encounter and the organisational work required to deliver it.

## When to use

Use service blueprints when designing or improving services involving multiple touchpoints, staff roles, systems or organisational processes.

## Process

1. Define the service and scenario being mapped.
2. Identify the user's major actions over time.
3. Add visible touchpoints and frontstage interactions.
4. Map backstage activities supporting those interactions.
5. Add relevant systems, processes and supporting actors.
6. Draw dependencies between layers.
7. Identify failures, bottlenecks and handoffs.
8. Examine how proposed changes affect both experience and delivery.
9. Revise the blueprint as the service develops.

## Look out for

Do not confuse a service blueprint with a user journey: the blueprint includes organisational delivery as well as user experience.

Complex blueprints can become unreadable; define a clear scope.

Validate backstage processes with the people who actually perform them.

## References

- Shostack, G. L. (1984). “Designing Services That Deliver.” _Harvard Business Review_, 62(1), 133–139.
- Bitner, M. J., Ostrom, A. L. & Morgan, F. N. (2008). “Service Blueprinting.” _California Management Review_, 50(3), 66–94.

# Sketching

## Description

Sketching uses rapid visual representations to explore, externalise and communicate ideas. In design, sketches are not simply illustrations of decisions already made; they are tools for thinking through form, relationships, interactions and alternatives.

Their provisional quality makes ideas easier to change and supports exploration before detailed commitment.

## When to use

Use sketching throughout design whenever visualising an idea can help you think about it, compare alternatives or communicate it to others.

## Process

1. Define the question you are exploring.
2. Produce a rough representation quickly.
3. Focus on relationships and ideas rather than visual polish.
4. Create several alternatives.
5. Annotate important behaviours or assumptions.
6. Compare sketches and identify promising elements.
7. Combine or transform those elements.
8. Increase detail only as the questions become more specific.

## Look out for

Drawing skill should not determine the perceived quality of an idea.

A polished sketch can discourage change too early.

Use annotations where a drawing could otherwise imply functionality or certainty that has not been resolved.

## References

- Buxton, B. (2007). _Sketching User Experiences_. Morgan Kaufmann.
- Goldschmidt, G. (1991). “The Dialectics of Sketching.” _Creativity Research Journal_, 4(2), 123–143.

# Slow Design

## Description

Slow Design questions design practices centred on speed, novelty and short-term consumption. It encourages attention to duration, locality, reflection, participation, material lifecycles and the longer-term relationships people develop with designed things.

Rather than being a single procedural method, Slow Design is better understood as a set of principles through which design decisions and their consequences can be reconsidered.

## When to use

Use Slow Design as a critical lens when a project raises questions about sustainability, longevity, consumption, repair, local context or the pace at which products and services are produced and replaced.

## Process

1. Identify where speed, convenience or novelty shapes the current design.
2. Examine the product or service across a longer timeframe.
3. Consider material, social and environmental consequences.
4. Investigate opportunities for maintenance, adaptation and repair.
5. Consider local knowledge, production and participation.
6. Explore interactions that encourage reflection rather than automatic consumption.
7. Develop alternative propositions.
8. Evaluate them according to long-term as well as immediate value.

## Look out for

"Slow" does not simply mean inefficient or time-consuming.

Longer-lasting products are not automatically sustainable if their materials or systems remain harmful.

Consider who has the time and resources to participate in slower practices; slowness can carry different costs for different people.

## References

- Strauss, C. F. & Fuad-Luke, A. (2008). “The Slow Design Principles.”
- Fuad-Luke, A. (2009). _Design Activism_. Earthscan.

# Spatial Mapping

## Description

Spatial mapping records how activities, people, objects or experiences are distributed and connected within a physical environment. Designers may map movement, occupation, interaction, visibility, obstacles, resources or moments of activity.

The resulting representation helps make relationships between behaviour and space visible.

## When to use

Use spatial mapping when physical arrangement, movement or environmental conditions influence an experience.

## Process

1. Define the environment and question.
2. Obtain or create a simple plan of the space.
3. Decide what activities or features will be recorded.
4. Observe the space over an appropriate period.
5. Mark locations, movements and events on the map.
6. Use layers or notation to distinguish different observations.
7. Compare patterns across times or participants.
8. Identify spatial constraints and opportunities for further investigation.

## Look out for

Short observation periods may capture unusual rather than typical patterns.

Mapping people's locations can create privacy concerns.

Avoid assuming that visible movement explains why people behave as they do; combine mapping with other research where necessary.

## References

- Zeisel, J. (2006). _Inquiry by Design_, rev. ed. W. W. Norton.

# Speculative Storytelling

## Description

Speculative storytelling uses narrative to explore possible situations, futures and consequences through characters and events. Stories allow designers to examine how broad changes might be experienced in everyday life and to connect technological or systemic propositions with social relationships and values.

The aim is not prediction but structured imagination.

## When to use

Use speculative storytelling when future conditions are too abstract to discuss productively or when a project needs to explore multiple perspectives on possible change.

## Process

1. Identify the issue or change being explored.
2. Establish a time and setting.
3. Define the conditions that differ from the present.
4. Create characters with different relationships to those conditions.
5. Develop an everyday situation through which the future becomes visible.
6. Introduce tensions, consequences and adaptations.
7. Compare alternative stories.
8. Use the narratives to identify assumptions, questions and design implications.

## Look out for

Avoid treating one story as _the_ future.

Characters should not exist merely as devices for explaining technology.

Consider who benefits, who carries risk and whose perspective remains absent.

## References

- Candy, S. (2010). _The Futures of Everyday Life_. University of Hawai'i at Mānoa.
- Dunne, A. & Raby, F. (2013). _Speculative Everything_. MIT Press.

# Speed Testing

## Description

Speed testing uses short, focused rounds of evaluation to expose obvious problems and compare design alternatives before investing in more extensive testing. The emphasis is on rapid learning and iteration rather than comprehensive validation.

A small number of targeted sessions can reveal significant usability or comprehension issues early enough for designers to change direction cheaply.

## When to use

Use speed testing when a design is changing rapidly and the team needs quick evidence to inform the next iteration.

## Process

1. Identify the most important uncertainty.
2. Create a prototype capable of investigating it.
3. Define one or two realistic tasks or prompts.
4. Recruit relevant participants where possible.
5. Run short sessions focused on observable behaviour.
6. Record significant difficulties and misunderstandings.
7. Revise the design immediately where appropriate.
8. Test again with subsequent participants.
9. Reserve broader claims for more systematic evaluation.

## Look out for

Speed should not remove informed consent or responsible participant treatment.

Very small samples are useful for discovering problems, not estimating how common those problems are.

Avoid changing the design after every isolated comment without considering the underlying evidence.

## References

- Krug, S. (2010). _Rocket Surgery Made Easy_. New Riders.
- Nielsen, J. (1993). _Usability Engineering_. Academic Press.

# STEP Method

## Description

STEP is used as a structured prompt for examining the broader context surrounding a design problem rather than considering the immediate product or user alone. It directs attention toward larger social, technological, economic and political conditions that may influence the project.

The framework can help designers recognise external forces, constraints and emerging changes that would otherwise remain outside a narrowly framed brief.

## When to use

Use STEP during early contextual research, strategic design or futures work when wider environmental conditions may substantially affect a project.

## Process

1. Define the issue or design context.
2. Investigate relevant social conditions and changes.
3. Investigate technological developments and dependencies.
4. Investigate economic conditions, incentives and constraints.
5. Investigate political, regulatory and institutional conditions.
6. Record evidence and uncertainties within each area.
7. Identify relationships between developments across categories.
8. Translate significant findings into implications or questions for the project.

## Look out for

Categories are prompts rather than independent systems; significant issues often cross several areas.

Avoid filling categories with generic trends that have no demonstrated relevance to the project.

Different versions of environmental scanning frameworks use different category labels, so define the version being used.

## References

- Aguilar, F. J. (1967). _Scanning the Business Environment_. Macmillan.
- Tomitsch, M. et al. (2021). _Design. Think. Make. Break. Repeat_. BIS Publishers.

# Storyboarding

## Description

Storyboarding represents an experience as a sequence of illustrated frames. Borrowed from filmmaking, the technique helps designers show how a situation unfolds over time, including context, actions, interactions and transitions.

Storyboards are particularly useful for communicating experiences that extend beyond a single screen or object.

## When to use

Use storyboarding when the timing and sequence of an experience matter, when communicating a scenario to others, or when exploring how a concept fits into everyday activity.

## Process

1. Define the scenario and central actor.
2. Identify the beginning and intended outcome.
3. Break the experience into significant moments.
4. Sketch each moment as a separate frame.
5. Show relevant context, people and artefacts.
6. Add brief annotations or dialogue where needed.
7. Review the sequence for missing transitions or assumptions.
8. Create alternative sequences where useful.

## Look out for

A smooth storyboard can conceal friction and uncertainty.

Include moments before and after the direct interaction with the design.

Drawing quality is less important than whether the sequence communicates clearly.

## References

- Truong, K. N., Hayes, G. R. & Abowd, G. D. (2006). “Storyboarding: An Empirical Determination of Best Practices and Effective Guidelines.” _DIS 2006_.

# Systems Mapping

## Description

Systems mapping visualises components of a complex situation and the relationships between them. Depending on the purpose, maps may include actors, resources, institutions, causal relationships, flows and feedback loops.

The method helps designers move beyond isolated problems and consider how outcomes emerge from interconnected conditions.

## When to use

Use systems mapping when a challenge involves multiple actors and interacting causes and when intervening in one part of the situation may create consequences elsewhere.

## Process

1. Define a provisional boundary for the system.
2. Identify significant actors, elements and resources.
3. Place them on a shared mapping surface.
4. Draw relationships and flows between elements.
5. Identify reinforcing and balancing relationships where relevant.
6. Look for clusters, dependencies and feedback.
7. Identify missing perspectives and challenge the system boundary.
8. Use the map to locate questions, leverage points and potential consequences of intervention.
9. Revise the map as understanding develops.

## Look out for

A system map is a model constructed for a purpose, not a complete representation of reality.

Boundaries determine what becomes visible.

Avoid equating visual complexity with analytical depth.

## References

- Meadows, D. H. (2008). _Thinking in Systems_. Chelsea Green.
- Jones, P. & Van Ael, K. (2022). _Design Journeys through Complex Systems_. BIS Publishers.

# Task Flow

## Description

A task flow represents the sequence of actions required to complete a particular goal. It focuses on the path through a defined task and can include user actions, system responses and decision points.

Task flows help designers examine whether necessary steps are understandable, appropriately ordered and unnecessarily complex.

## When to use

Use task flows when designing or reviewing a specific interaction such as registration, booking, purchasing, submitting information or completing a workflow.

## Process

1. Define the user goal.
2. Establish the starting and completion conditions.
3. List the actions currently or potentially required.
4. Arrange them in sequence.
5. Add decisions, alternative paths and system responses.
6. Identify unnecessary steps and dependencies.
7. Compare the flow against research and technical constraints.
8. Revise the sequence.
9. Prototype and test important flows with users.

## Look out for

Do not design only the ideal path.

Include errors, interruptions, reversals and recovery where relevant.

A logically efficient flow may still be confusing when represented through an interface, so flow diagrams do not replace usability testing.

## References

- Hackos, J. T. & Redish, J. C. (1998). _User and Task Analysis for Interface Design_. Wiley.

# Thematic Analysis

## Description

Thematic analysis is a qualitative analytical method for identifying and interpreting patterns of meaning across a body of data. Researchers become familiar with the material, code relevant features, develop candidate themes and progressively refine their interpretation.

A theme is more than a frequently mentioned topic: it captures a meaningful pattern relevant to the research question.

## When to use

Use thematic analysis when working with qualitative material such as interviews, diary entries, open survey responses or observational notes and when the research question concerns recurring patterns of experience or meaning.

## Process

1. Become familiar with the complete dataset.
2. Record initial observations.
3. Code relevant features systematically.
4. Compare codes across the material.
5. Group related codes into candidate themes.
6. Review themes against the coded extracts and wider dataset.
7. Refine the boundaries and meaning of each theme.
8. Name themes clearly.
9. Develop an interpretation supported by appropriate evidence.

## Look out for

Themes do not simply "emerge" independently of the researcher; analysis involves interpretation.

Frequency alone does not determine importance.

Maintain a traceable relationship between claims, codes and original material.

## References

- Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2006). “Using Thematic Analysis in Psychology.” _Qualitative Research in Psychology_, 3(2), 77–101.
- Braun, V. & Clarke, V. (2022). _Thematic Analysis: A Practical Guide_. Sage.

# Thing Ethnography

## Description

Thing ethnography investigates everyday practices from the perspective of objects and other non-human participants. Rather than treating things as passive props around human activity, the method asks how objects participate in relationships, routines and infrastructures.

Approaches can include attaching sensors or cameras to objects, tracing their movement and interactions, or deliberately adopting a "thing perspective" to expose relationships that conventional human-centred observation might overlook.

## When to use

Use thing ethnography when relationships between people, objects and infrastructures are important and when a conventional user-centred perspective appears too narrow.

It is particularly relevant to connected products, Internet of Things research and more-than-human design.

## Process

1. Identify a thing whose relationships are relevant to the design question.
2. Map the people, objects and infrastructures it encounters.
3. Determine how traces of those interactions might be documented.
4. Collect material from or about the thing's situated perspective.
5. Examine patterns of movement, use and connection.
6. Compare the thing perspective with human accounts.
7. Identify relationships that conventional observation may have overlooked.
8. Use these insights to reconsider the boundaries of the design problem.

## Look out for

Objects do not literally provide human-style accounts of experience; the "thing perspective" remains a methodological construction.

Sensor-based observation can create significant privacy concerns.

Avoid using more-than-human language merely as metaphor when the analysis remains entirely human-centred.

## References

- Giaccardi, E., Cila, N., Speed, C. & Caldwell, M. (2016). “Thing Ethnography: Doing Design Research with Non-Humans.” _Proceedings of DIS 2016_, 377–387. DOI: 10.1145/2901790.2901905.

# Thing from the Future

## Description

The Thing from the Future is a futures-oriented ideation game in which prompts are combined to imagine an artefact from a possible future. Constraints commonly describe elements such as the type of future, the time horizon, the kind of object and an emotional or thematic quality.

The random combination pushes participants away from familiar predictions and toward a wider range of future possibilities.

## When to use

Use the method when a group needs to expand its imagination of possible futures, begin a futures workshop or generate speculative artefacts quickly.

## Process

1. Establish the issue or domain being explored.
2. Generate or draw a set of future-oriented prompts.
3. Combine prompts to create an unusual design constraint.
4. Individually or collaboratively imagine an artefact responding to it.
5. Sketch and describe how the artefact works.
6. Explain what future conditions would make the artefact meaningful.
7. Share propositions across the group.
8. Compare the assumptions and worlds implied by different artefacts.

## Look out for

The artefact is a doorway into discussing a future, not simply an unusual product-design exercise.

Randomness can produce superficial ideas unless participants develop the surrounding social context.

Distinguish imaginative possibility from prediction.

## References

- Candy, S. & Watson, J. (2015). _The Thing from the Future_. Situation Lab.

# Think Aloud

## Description

Think-aloud testing asks participants to verbalise what they are noticing, expecting and trying to do while interacting with a design. Their commentary is considered alongside observed behaviour to help researchers understand where expectations and interface behaviour diverge.

It is commonly used during usability evaluation.

## When to use

Use think aloud when evaluating an interface or prototype and when understanding participants' interpretation of controls, content and feedback is important.

## Process

1. Define realistic tasks.
2. Explain that the design rather than the participant is being evaluated.
3. Demonstrate the type of verbalisation requested if necessary.
4. Ask the participant to begin the task.
5. Observe behaviour without guiding them toward the solution.
6. Prompt them neutrally to keep talking if they become silent.
7. Record significant actions, comments and difficulties.
8. Debrief after the task.
9. Analyse behaviour and verbal reports together.

## Look out for

Thinking aloud changes the activity and can slow performance.

Participants cannot necessarily verbalise every cognitive process.

Avoid turning the session into an interview by asking explanatory questions during every action.

## References

- Ericsson, K. A. & Simon, H. A. (1993). _Protocol Analysis: Verbal Reports as Data_, rev. ed. MIT Press.
- Nielsen, J. (1993). _Usability Engineering_. Academic Press.

# Usability Testing

## Description

Usability testing involves observing representative participants attempting realistic tasks with a product, service or prototype. Researchers look for difficulties, misunderstandings and successful interactions and use these observations to improve the design.

The emphasis is on what happens during use rather than asking participants only whether they like the design.

## When to use

Use usability testing whenever a design is sufficiently developed for people to attempt meaningful tasks. Testing can begin with paper prototypes and continue through implementation.

## Process

1. Define the usability questions.
2. Identify representative participants.
3. Create realistic tasks without revealing how to complete them.
4. Prepare the prototype and test environment.
5. Establish consent and explain the session.
6. Observe participants attempting each task.
7. Avoid helping unless the study design requires intervention.
8. Ask follow-up questions after relevant interactions.
9. Analyse recurring problems and their severity.
10. Revise and test again.

## Look out for

Usability testing is not simply asking participants for opinions.

Participants should not feel that their ability is being tested.

A small qualitative study can identify problems but does not provide precise population-level performance estimates.

Include participants with relevant accessibility needs rather than treating accessibility as a separate final check.

## References

- Rubin, J. & Chisnell, D. (2008). _Handbook of Usability Testing_, 2nd ed. Wiley.
- Nielsen, J. (1993). _Usability Engineering_. Academic Press.

# User Flow

## Description

A user flow visualises the routes a person may take through a product or service to reach one or more goals. Unlike a simple task flow, it may include multiple entry points, decisions and alternative paths through the system.

User flows help designers examine how individual screens, states or touchpoints connect into a coherent experience.

## When to use

Use user flows when designing navigation and interaction across multiple states or screens and when several routes through an experience need to be considered.

## Process

1. Define the relevant user goal.
2. Identify possible entry points.
3. Map major actions and system responses.
4. Add decisions and alternative paths.
5. Include completion, exit and error states.
6. Review the flow for unnecessary complexity.
7. Compare it against research and scenarios.
8. Translate the flow into wireframes or prototypes.
9. Test whether actual users can navigate the intended paths.

## Look out for

User flows can imply orderly behaviour that does not reflect real use.

Include returning users, interruptions and recovery where relevant.

Do not optimise every user toward the same route when people have legitimately different goals.

## References

- Garrett, J. J. (2011). _The Elements of User Experience_, 2nd ed. New Riders.

# User Journey Mapping

## Description

A user journey map represents an experience across a sequence of stages or touchpoints from the perspective of a person pursuing a goal. It can combine actions, interactions, thoughts, difficulties and contextual information to show how an experience develops over time.

Journey maps help teams see connections between moments that organisational structures may otherwise treat separately.

## When to use

Use journey mapping when an experience crosses multiple touchpoints, channels or periods of time and when the team needs a shared view of the end-to-end experience.

## Process

1. Define the user, goal and scope of the journey.
2. Gather relevant research.
3. Identify major stages.
4. Map actions and touchpoints across those stages.
5. Add evidence about needs, questions and difficulties.
6. Identify transitions and moments where responsibility changes.
7. Mark significant opportunities or breakdowns.
8. Validate the map with further evidence.
9. Use it to prioritise design questions or service improvements.

## Look out for

Journey maps created in workshops without user research often represent organisational assumptions rather than user journeys.

Emotional curves can create false precision if feelings have not actually been investigated.

Avoid forcing diverse experiences into one supposedly universal journey.

## References

- Kalbach, J. (2016). _Mapping Experiences_. O'Reilly.
- Stickdorn, M. et al. (2018). _This Is Service Design Doing_. O'Reilly.

# User Profiles

## Description

User profiles summarise relevant characteristics of people or groups who interact with a product or service. Profiles may describe experience, behaviours, contexts, capabilities, constraints and goals without constructing the fictional character typically associated with a persona.

They can provide a concise way to communicate meaningful differences within a user population.

## When to use

Use user profiles when research reveals important user characteristics that should inform design but a full persona format would add unnecessary narrative detail.

## Process

1. Gather research about relevant users.
2. Identify characteristics that materially influence the design.
3. Group related patterns carefully.
4. Describe each profile using evidence-based characteristics.
5. Include relevant capabilities, contexts and constraints.
6. Avoid unnecessary demographic information.
7. Compare design requirements across profiles.
8. Revise profiles as research develops.

## Look out for

Profiles can become stereotypes if based primarily on demographics or assumptions.

Do not treat categories as mutually exclusive unless evidence supports doing so.

Focus on differences that have consequences for design.

## References

- Hackos, J. T. & Redish, J. C. (1998). _User and Task Analysis for Interface Design_. Wiley.

# Value Proposition

## Description

A value proposition articulates why a particular group might find a product, service or intervention valuable. It connects the circumstances, needs or goals of that group with the benefits a proposed offering intends to create.

Developing a value proposition can help teams make assumptions explicit and examine whether a concept addresses something people actually value.

## When to use

Use value proposition work when developing or refining a concept and when the relationship between the proposed offering and the needs it addresses remains unclear.

## Process

1. Define the group or situation being addressed.
2. Identify relevant goals, needs and difficulties from research.
3. Describe the proposed product or service.
4. Explain what benefit it intends to create.
5. Identify existing alternatives.
6. State why the proposition might be meaningfully different.
7. Mark assumptions requiring validation.
8. Test the proposition through research and prototypes.
9. Revise the statement as evidence develops.

## Look out for

A value proposition is a hypothesis until supported by evidence.

Avoid inventing customer needs simply to justify an existing solution.

Value may be social, public, environmental or experiential as well as commercial.

## References

- Osterwalder, A. et al. (2014). _Value Proposition Design_. Wiley.

# Video Prototyping

## Description

Video prototyping uses recorded or edited video to represent how a proposed product, service or interaction might work in context. People, props, interfaces and environments can be combined to simulate functionality that does not yet exist.

Video is particularly effective for representing interactions involving movement, time, multiple devices or changing environments.

## When to use

Use video prototyping when an experience is difficult to communicate through static sketches and when implementing the underlying technology would be unnecessary or expensive at the current stage.

## Process

1. Define the interaction or question to represent.
2. Develop a short scenario.
3. Storyboard the significant moments.
4. Decide what can be performed, mocked up or simulated.
5. Prepare simple props and interfaces.
6. Record the scenario.
7. Edit only enough to communicate the interaction clearly.
8. Review the video with collaborators or participants.
9. Use feedback to revise the concept before implementation.

## Look out for

Video can make an unbuilt concept appear more complete and feasible than it really is.

Clearly distinguish simulated behaviour from functioning technology.

Avoid investing heavily in production quality when the concept itself remains uncertain.

## References

- Mackay, W. E. (1988). “Video Prototyping: A Technique for Developing Hypermedia Systems.” _CHI '88_.
- Tomitsch, M. et al. (2021). _Design. Think. Make. Break. Repeat_. BIS Publishers.

# Wireframing

## Description

Wireframing represents the structure and organisation of a digital interface without requiring finished visual styling. Wireframes typically show content hierarchy, controls, navigation and relationships between interface elements.

Their reduced visual detail allows designers to examine structure and interaction before typography, imagery and visual identity dominate discussion.

## When to use

Use wireframes when moving from flows and requirements toward interface layouts and when the main questions concern hierarchy, navigation and interaction rather than final appearance.

## Process

1. Define the user goal and relevant content.
2. Review the task or user flow.
3. Identify the elements required on each screen or state.
4. Arrange those elements according to hierarchy and task priority.
5. Connect screens to represent important interactions.
6. Create alternative layouts for uncertain areas.
7. Review the wireframes against scenarios and accessibility requirements.
8. Prototype and test important interactions.
9. Increase visual fidelity only when structural questions have been addressed.

## Look out for

Wireframes can prematurely lock teams into familiar screen layouts.

Placeholder content can hide genuine content-design problems; use realistic content where possible.

Low visual fidelity does not remove the need to consider accessibility, responsive behaviour and information hierarchy.

# Reference Notes

The references throughout this manuscript serve two purposes:

1. to identify established or originating literature where a method has a recognisable scholarly history; and
2. to provide students with credible starting points for learning beyond the short reference entry.

Not every design method has a single canonical origin. Some have evolved across professional practice, HCI, design research, engineering, management and futures studies. References should therefore not be interpreted as claims that a particular author "invented" a method unless the historical record supports that claim.

# Editorial notes for review

The manuscript deliberately avoids assigning methods rigidly to stages of a design process. A method's appropriateness depends on the question being investigated, the maturity of the project and the context in which it is being used.

Descriptions also avoid presenting all entries as equivalent kinds of "method". The collection includes research methods, analytical techniques, generative activities, representations, evaluation techniques, frameworks and broader design approaches. They can coexist in a practical reference library without implying methodological equivalence.

The **When to use** sections are intended to support method selection rather than prescribe a fixed process.

The **Look out for** sections are deliberately substantive. They identify epistemic, ethical, accessibility or practical limitations rather than generic advice such as "remember to involve users".

The **Process** sections should be interpreted as teachable starting sequences. Students should be encouraged to explain how and why they adapt a method to their particular project rather than being assessed on procedural conformity.
